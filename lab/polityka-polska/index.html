Polska wyjaśniona rozdział po rozdziale

Report z cyklu badań nad polską polityką publiczną (karty C01-C16, zadania 1100-1115 i 2087 z października i listopada 2026): szesnaście samodzielnych analiz danych publicznych — GUS BDL, Eurostat, GIOŚ, NIK, OECD, PSE, CBOS, Eurobarometer — zebranych w jeden dokument, rozdział po rozdziale.

Zasada neutralności wspólna wszystkim rozdziałom: każdy wynik jest albo FAKTEM (źródło + rok + jednostka), albo wyraźnie oznaczoną ZAMIANĄ KORZYŚCI (TRADE-OFF), albo OCENĄ SUBIEKTYWNĄ (VALUE JUDGEMENT). Żaden ranking nie jest przedstawiony jako obiektywny — każdy wynika z podanego obok wektora wag. Komórki, których źródła nie opublikowały, oznaczono MISSING i nigdy nie uzupełniono założeniem.

Struktura: rozdziały 1-8 to wyjściowe obrazy stanu (baseline) ośmiu domen; rozdziały 9-14 to oceny wariantów działań (appraisal) w sześciu portfelach; rozdział 15 porównuje klasy miast. Załącznik A przewartościowuje wszystkie oceny przy trzech jawnie podanych wektorach wag (robustność). Źródła, które rozdział wylicza wprost, zebrano we wspólnej bibliografii na końcu; źródła podane inline pozostają w treści rozdziałów. Treść rozdziałów przeniesiono bez zmian: niniejsze zestawienie dodaje tylko nagłówki, spis treści i bibliografię.

4. TRADE-OFF notes (FACT-based, no ranking implied)

  • East-west economy gradient is real but narrow outside the capital: GDP/cap PPS spans 55 (PL81) to 157 (PL91) with EU27=100 and PL≈78-82 in the centre-west; without PL91 the national spread is 55-83.
  • Demography runs the other way: the economically weaker east (PL82, PL81, PL84) shows the lowest old-age dependency (33.5-37.1) — driven by higher fertility and out-migration of elderly-care-aged cohorts — while Łódzkie (38.7) and Świętokrzyskie (40.0) age fastest. Net-migration estimates are positive only for PL91 (5.2), PL63 (2.2), PL51 (1.5), PL21 (1.25).
  • Water losses span 11.5 % (Pomorskie) to 22.9 % (Małopolskie); the BDL whole-Mazowieckie row (PL92w) is 12.5 % in 2024 (mid-range; the 10.9 % figure is the 2025 value) and cannot be split to PL91/PL92. Pomorskie 11.5 % is the lowest 2024 value.
  • Health: cancer SDR is lowest in Podkarpackie (221.8) and Lubelskie (244.4), highest in Śląskie (284.9) and Pomorskie (284.9); life expectancy (M/F mean) is highest in Podkarpackie (79.83) and Małopolskie (79.75), lowest in Łódzkie (77.52). The east scores well on health despite scoring poorly on the economy — any aggregate that hides this is misleading.
  • Education: tertiary attainment ranges 31.2-42.8 among the 16 non-capital regions and 64.7 in PL91 — the capital pulls 22+ points above the rest; the split makes PL92 (31.8) look like the east although it surrounds the capital.
  • Incomplete rows are optimistic: PL91, PL92 (3 missing indicators each) and PL43 (1 missing) are scored on renormalized remaining weights (weight mass 0.40-0.97 depending on vector); their ranks should be read with that caveat, e.g. PL91's environmental rank 1 rests on 40 % of that vector's weights.

5. What the ranking changes when weights change — one-line answer

The podium (PL91, PL63, PL41) and the last place (PL72) are weight-stable; everything in between is weight-sensitive, with rank shifts up to 12 places (PL21) driven mainly by the water-losses weight.

1. Retention vs the 15 % target

  • FACT: Government portal (gov.pl, "Zasoby wodne i retencja", Ministry of Infrastructure programme page, fetched 2026-10-30): "Obecnie w naszym kraju dzięki retencji gromadzimy około 7,5 % objętości średniorocznego odpływu rzecznego." and "Szacuje się, że realne możliwości retencji ... wynoszą w Polsce 15% średniego rocznego odpływu." — current ~7.5 % of mean annual outflow retained, real capability ~15 % (the doubling target of the Programme to counter water shortage, PPNW).
  • FACT (contested figure, second independent source): Grygoruk, M., "Licznik i mianownik polskiej retencji, czyli 15% odpływu", wodnesprawy.pl, published 2023-03-23 (fetched 2026-10-30): taking reservoir capacity plus fish ponds (725.3 mln m3), restored channel retention and soil retention on 4.7 mln ha of drained land, "retencjonujemy aktualnie w Polsce około 15% odpływu, a więc niemal dwa razy więcej niż wynoszą najnowsze szacunki PGW Wody Polskie" — the author argues "cel 15% retencji wskazywany w Programie Przeciwdziałania Niedoborowi Wody został już osiągnięty". The two documents disagree by a factor of ~2 (7.5 % vs ~13-15 %); the difference is the definition of counted retention (artificial reservoirs only vs all hydrotechnical + natural retention).
  • Denominator (both sources): mean annual outflow from Poland. gov.pl gives ~62 km3/yr (1951-2000 average range 37.5-90 km3 per the portal); Grygoruk gives ~47 km3/yr as the 30-year mean from recent river gauging. The choice of numerator and denominator is the whole argument (TRADE-OFF: official metric understates retention including natural storage; the broader metric makes the target look already met and could justify defunding retention investment).
  • Status: retention figure has two sources of different kind (government portal + independent hydrologist), both quoted above. The statutory anchor (M.P. 2019 poz. 941 "Założenia do programu rozwoju retencji", ISAP PDF) could not be fetched — ISAP serves a bot-protection redirect loop (recorded in commands.log); single source: gov.pl for the official 7.5 %/15 % wording. 5. Cost-per-m3 sketch of loss reduction (CEA per strategies.md #2)

Sketch only, no monetised health/scarcity effects (card says data thin). Anchor figures (FACT, sourced): losses 2024 = 308.7 mln m3/yr (BDL); NIK 2025: lost water costs 8.8-12.1 % of the water price; NIK 2018: monitoring investment paid back ~3x in one year at one utility. 2. Four levers: mechanism, evidence, fiscal cost, distribution, time-to-effect

Evidence grades used: High = number reproduced from an official statistic by two paths or a peer-reviewed result with identified identification; Medium = single official document/audit with numbers; Low = single secondary or advocacy source, or method not published. Causality caveat that applies to every lever: no lever below was evaluated in Poland by a design that isolates its causal effect on completions (no Polish WZ/plan-reform diff-in-diff was found in the fetched sources). Grades grade the evidence of the direction and size range, not Polish causal proof; the completions divergence in §1a is descriptive, not a lever effect.

Lever 1 — Zoning: raise MPZP/plan coverage (binding plans instead of WZ decisions)

  • Mechanism: a binding plan converts land from case-by-case WZ permission to as-of-right development; Gyourko–Molloy's reviewed literature (regulation ⇒ less construction) implies its expansion works through certainty and permitted density. In Poland the substitute instrument (WZ) is demonstrably weak: 36% of WZ housing decisions in NIK's audit stood on land the city's own studium did not designate for development (NIK 2023, quote above).
  • Effect claim (traced): expanding plan coverage raises the share of development that can proceed without discretionary permission; international evidence says restrictive zoning lowers construction (Gyourko–Molloy 2015; Medium–High, multi-study review). No Polish study found that quantifies completions gained per percentage point of coverage (documented absence, see handoff) — the city table is graded on coverage levels and on the observed completions pattern, not on a Polish causal estimate.
  • Fiscal cost: plan-making is the cost: 10,475 projects in preparation nationally (BDL P2850 2025), 36.0% drafted >3 years — gminas pay planners/expropriation-free planning but bear staff time for years; NIK 2023 documents the delay side of the same cost. No zł-per-plan figure is published in the fetched sources (not published, handoff).
  • Distribution (TRADE-OFF): renters and builders gain tradable certainty; owners of land newly given buildable status gain windfall land value; municipalities gain plan control but lose the case-by-case bargaining WZ gave them. Owners near new development can lose amenity.
  • Time-to-effect: slow — plan drafting alone takes ">4 lata" for a large share (NIK 2023; 36% of open projects >3 years, BDL 2025), plus statutory consultation; first completions realistically 3–6+ years after a city starts.
  • City grading (coverage measured 2025, within-break): Gdańsk 53.8% and Kraków 25.7% enter the lever already mid-way; Warszawa ~16–18% is the largest exposed big-city stock (largest completions base, plan coverage lowest among the four with high completions); Poznań 14.4% (2024 method) doubled vs 2023-method 6.9% — cannot be split across the break; Łódź ~11.6% lowest. Effect-on-completions evidence grade: Low per city (no Polish causal estimate); state-of-implementation evidence: Medium (official survey).

Lever 2 — Land release: municipal/state land onto the market

  • Mechanism: public owners control large land banks; selling/leasing serviced land raises the quantity of land facing builders.   • Effect claim (traced): direction (more building land ⇒ more completions) follows from the same supply literature (Gyourko–Molloy 2015); size in Poland unpublished. Grade: Low.
  • Fiscal cost: opportunity cost — land sold below market (or with affordable-housing covenants) is municipal revenue forgone; the 2025 draft "ustawa podażowa" (search summaries, single source) would ease conversion (restored użytkowanie wieczyste, extra storey under 30 m via simplified procedure) — a regulatory transfer, not a budget outlay.

  • Time-to-effect: medium — serviced city land can deliver completions in 2–4 years (no published Polish number; engineering lead-time reading, marked as interpretation).

5. Reading guide (one paragraph, non-technical)

Imagine each action is graded only against actions fighting the same problem (air, water, energy), because the appraisals measured them in different units. Each action gets a 0-1 grade per question: how much good does a zloty buy, how many people does it help, and what does it do for climate and security. Then we grade with three different value systems: one that cares mostly about the budget, one mostly about people, one mostly about climate and security, and we run each action at its best-case and worst-case published numbers. Three actions come out near the top no matter which value system or which case: replacing the dirtiest home heating (clean-heat subsidies), plugging water leaks in networks, and building renewables before waiting for other power plants. Everything else either stays second in its field (deep renovation, land retention — and retention's good grade leans on plans that are only 10% built), swaps places with a neighbour when you change the value system (grid-first vs coal-hold), or — for nine of the eighteen actions, including the whole demography and housing sets — cannot be graded at all from published numbers, which is itself the most important result for the owner: those portfolios need evaluation work before any ranking means anything. (The 2026-10-31 correction of the energy appraisal did not change any of these conclusions.)

7. What this analysis does not say

  • "Best in its appraisal" (score 1.0) is a within-domain statement, not a claim that AIR-A beats WAT-B in absolute terms — no published common unit exists across the six appraisals, and inventing one is exactly what the card forbids.
  • The K1/K2/K3 split maps each appraisal's own metrics; appraisals measured different things (C09 deaths, C10 water volumes, C12 CO2), so K2/K3 availability is uneven by construction, not by judgement.
  • Ranges are the appraisals' own; low/high impact worlds take the ends at face value (WAT-A's fav end is plan capacity — flagged in §4.4).
  • Ranking a NOT RANKABLE option would require either new Polish effect sizes (C13, C14), a commensurable outcome scale for C11, or value judgements the card forbids this task from making.
